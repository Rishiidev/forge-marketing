If someone searches "[your service] near me" right now, what do they see? For most local businesses, the honest answer is: a Google Business Profile with a few photos, a handful of reviews, and not much else. That profile is often the very first impression a customer forms of your business — before they ever see your storefront, your website, or talk to anyone on your team.

Here's what actually moves the needle, in the order it matters.

## 1. Get your category right — then add secondary categories

Your primary category is the single biggest factor in which searches you show up for. A "hair salon" and a "beauty salon" are treated differently by Google's local algorithm, even though a customer might use the terms interchangeably.

Pick the primary category that most precisely matches what you do, then add every relevant secondary category Google offers. A dentist who also does teeth whitening should list both. This costs nothing and takes five minutes, and most businesses never revisit it after setup.

## 2. Fill in every field, not just the required ones

Business hours, service area, attributes ("wheelchair accessible", "women-led"), a phone number, and a real website link — each one is a small trust signal, and each one is a small ranking signal. An incomplete profile doesn't just look unfinished to a customer; it also gives Google less to match your business against a search.

## 3. Add real photos, regularly

Profiles with recent, real photos consistently get more attention than ones with a single logo image from years ago. Photos of your actual space, your team, and your work signal that the business is active and genuinely matches what it claims to be. Stock photos do the opposite — they're easy to spot and they undermine the trust you're trying to build.

## 4. Treat your Google reviews as a conversation, not a scoreboard

Star rating matters, but *how you respond* matters almost as much. A business that replies to reviews — good and bad — reads as active and accountable. A business that never responds reads as absent, even if the ratings are good.

Ask happy customers to leave a review right after a good experience, while it's fresh. Never offer anything in exchange for a review — it violates Google's policy and it's the kind of shortcut that erodes trust the moment a customer notices it.

## 5. Use Google Posts for anything time-sensitive

Posts (offers, updates, events) show directly on your profile and give Google fresh content to index. They're free, they take two minutes, and most competitors in most local categories simply don't use them — which makes it an easy way to look more active than the business next door.

## 6. Answer the questions people are already asking

The Q&A section on your profile is public and anyone can post a question — including, sometimes, a competitor. Seed it yourself with the three or four questions you get asked most often ("Do you take walk-ins?", "Is there parking?"), answered clearly. An empty Q&A section is a missed opportunity; an unanswered one looks neglected.

## 7. Know what a Google Business Profile can't do for you

This is the part most guides skip. A well-optimized profile gets you found and gets you the click — but it can't show a customer your work in detail, explain your pricing clearly, or make the case for why *you* specifically, the way a real website can. Google itself treats a linked website as a trust signal, not a formality.

### The honest summary

None of this requires a marketing budget. It requires about an hour, done properly, and then five minutes a month to keep it current.
